Samina Khatoon is pregnant again after six children, one of whom died in childhood, and three miscarriages. Worn out, she complains of pelvic pain, leg pain and headaches.

All three times she miscarried, she could not afford to see a doctor, so she just stayed at home, letting the miscarriages happen. Her last birth was through a C-section, after which, she needed blood and was admitted to the hospital for three days — each day costing up to 10,000 rupees, about $100 in all. The bill was too much for a labourer’s family in Sindh Province, Pakistan, so Khatoon and her husband had to borrow to cover it.

“If there is only one person earning in a house, then how can a woman raise her five children properly?” she said.

Khatoon wanted to have her tubes tied after her last kid. But the choice wasn't hers. It was her husband’s. He, however, was hoping for more children, so he denied her a tubal ligation, despite Khatoon’s increasingly dangerous pregnancies and the family’s extreme poverty.  

"If Allah has fed these kids, Allah will feed the rest too,” Khatoon’s husband told her, ending the conversation.

Without her husband's consent, a doctor would not even consider her case. There is no law in Sindh Province, or in most places in Pakistan, that says a woman can’t make decisions about contraception for herself. And yet, throughout the country, in villages as well as in cities, doctors follow an unsaid policy of first obtaining a husband’s approval for tubal ligations.

This not only strips women of a reproductive right, it gives husbands and physicians the power to exclude them from a decision about their own health. This practice puts women at risk of illness, injury and death by forcing them to carry more children than their bodies may be able to handle.

Tubal ligation is a 20-30-minute medical sterilization procedure, tying a woman’s fallopian tubes to permanently prevent pregnancy. It is generally referred to as “getting your tubes tied .”

After the birth of her last kid, Khatoon went on the pill.

“I wanted to rest with anti-pregnancy medication because I was not well,”she said at a reproductive health session near her home last winter.

Despite taking precautions, Khatoon got pregnant again. Satisfied with six children, her husband had agreed to let her have her tubes tied.

Five kids were enough

According to the most recent data available at the world bank, Pakistan has a higher maternal mortality rate (155 deaths per 100,000) than neighboring India, Bangladesh and Nepal. And the same can be said for its fertility rate (3.5 births per woman) and infant mortality rate(50 deaths per 1,000 births). Poverty is a reality for 45% of Pakistanis according to the most recent household survey done for 2018-19, due partly to a birth rate of 3.6 per woman that makes it the world’s fifth most populous country. According to the World Bank's October 2025 Poverty & Equity Brief, in addition to nearly half of all Pakistanis living below the poverty line, 16.5% are living in extreme poverty.

Advocates say tubal ligation, which is a permanent form of birth control, can reduce family sizes and birth rates, which also are higher than the rest of the region, at 28 per 1,000 children, meaning Pakistan consistently has the most births per 1,000 people compared to its neighbours.

Population management has become an existential threat for Pakistan. The country has a population growth rate of 2.40, highest in south Asia. It also means Pakistan will not be able to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals by 2030 if the trajectory does reduce, said Dr. Zeba Sathar, a senior associate and country director of the Population Council, a global nonprofit on reproductive health and population research, at the Pakistan Population Summit. She said “..We really need to declare some sort of human development emergency.”

.

Pakistan Population Summit held in December 2025 was focused on the urgency to tackle increasing population because of its direct impacts on the economy and welfare of people.

“The crisis in human development is reflected by the fact that almost all indicators – literacy, education, health, poverty, gender disparity … have been deteriorating in recent years,” Maleeha Lodhi, former permanent representative to the United Nations said.

In 2023, the guttmacher institute with population council did a study that revealed that Pakistan has approximately 6 million unwanted pregnancies out of which 3.8 million resulted in abortion. Just eliminating this, according to Sathar, could be a game changer for Pakistan.

And yet, obstetricians and gynecologists have presented a barrier to women who want that option, even though most of the ob/gyns in Pakistan are women.  

“When a woman is your wife, she becomes your property, so any decision related to her body has to be taken by the man. That's the thinking or that's the concept,”said Madiha Latif, Vice President Strategic Engagement and Innovation, Pathfinder, a nonprofit that advocatesfor women's sexual and reproductive rights as well as the educational and property rights routinely denied them in a patriarchal society.

Only the country’s most populous province, Punjab, where 40% of the country’s poor live, restricts a woman’s right to a tubal ligation by requiring both her and her husband’s consents. However, Dr Amna Rehman, who did her three month rotation in gynae and obstetrics during her year-long internship year at Holy Family hospital in Rawalpindi, considered part of Punjab, said they never had a woman sign the consent form. If the husband wasn't available, they had to get a male member of his family to sign. The patient’s consent was assumed. The consent form, she said, was created on a blank piece of paper, with the doctors handwriting and a line drawn for the husband’s signature.

Very few women in Punjab are accepting contraception after delivery, in large part because they can’t get permission from their husbands, said Sathar.

Several doctors in provinces where women can get their tubes tied legally without their husbands’ consents, said they thought the practice of getting a husband’s approval was based in law. Others were unsure. In fact, doctors are taught in medical school to never perform the procedure without the husband’s consent. It comes down to fear. Several doctors conceded that an angry husband might retaliate against a doctor or their patient.

Rehman said doctors need to do it to keep themselves safe because it is possible that someone shows up to the hospital and says that they were not informed.

Khero Dero, a village of roughly 7000, sits in interior Sindh in southern Pakistan. On an unusually hot day in December, Ali Hassan Mangi Trust volunteers visited families they have assisted in getting tubal ligations.Many families here and in surrounding villages work in rice or wheat fields while women also keep house and tend to large families.

Shahnawaz, 36, a farmer said  “ I wished to have 10-12 kids because that’s what everyone wishes for in our village..No one here thinks of having a few kids.” They have three sons and five daughters.

During his wife, Saniya’s, fifth pregnancy, she complained about feeling weak and asked her husband to let her get a tubal ligation. “I told my husband, I won’t give birth to more kids. These are enough”

Her job not only includes taking care of her children but also household chores like cooking, feeding animals, and washing clothes with hands.

“ I am one person,” she said . "How do I take care of all this alone?”

After their eldest daughter was married off, the entire responsibility of taking care of the house fell on her shoulders.

“But I didn't leave her alone,” Shahnawaz admitted,

Saniya faced pressure to have more kids not only from her husband but also her mother-in-law. Her husband says eight kids is a good compromise.

“If it was up to me,” Saniya said. “Five kids were enough.,”

Medical Paternalism

About 15 miles from Kher Dero, Dr. Saba Tunio sits at a Reproductive Health Services Center-A, which is a hospital unit set up by the Population Welfare Department of Sindh, aimed at providing reproductive healthcare in Larkana, the closest city to the village. Tunio lists the “pre-requisites” to getting a tubal ligation. In addition to medical conditions that might make the surgery risky, Tunio says. The patient’s family should be  “complete,” which means they have both male and female children and enough not to regret their decision in the future. Tunio also makes sure the couple has at least two male babies, so in case one of them dies, the couple is still left with one. If the couple has only one male child, even if the husband consents to the procedure, Tunio won’t do it.  

“We live in a male-dominanted society,” she reasons. “He won't be happy with just female babies.”

Dr Xaher Gul, a public health physician who has worked extensively in reproductive health, calls such reasoning “medical paternalism” or “service providers’ bias” because it’s based on the assumption that the patient doesn't know better.

“It is still very old school where doctors understand and are taught that they will have to make decisions for their patients,” he said.

The government of Pakistan does not specify the number of children a couple should have before they get contraception. “Thats not part of the policy. If anyone thinks that’s the case, they are misinformed,” he said.

Tunio said she won’t do a tubal ligation on a new mother because it often isn’t known for months if an infant has a disease that might shorten its life and make the mother regret her decision. And she won’t do it on a woman with only daughters because permanent contraception might make her husband divorce her to have a chance at having a boy. She said she has never seen a woman with only daughters ask for tubal ligation.

She narrated the story of a patient with one child, a daughter, who asked for a tubal ligation because things weren't going well between her and her husband. It was her husband’s second marriage so he already had several kids. Tunio refused to do the surgery but the woman was able to get it elsewhere. Things eventually improved between the couple and they returned to ask for a reversal of the procedure, which was not possible. For Tunio, that’s reason enough for the restrictions she imposes on her patients.

Some doctors don’t agree that multiple C-sections are harmful.

 “People are having five, six or even seven cesareans,” said Dr. Fouzia Amir, a gynecologist in Islamabad. “It depends on the tissue reaction. Sometimes their tissue reaction is such that cesarean is hard, but we still manage to do it. It’s not like you can't do more than three or four.”

Amir works at Shifa international hospital, a private facility and one of the leading hospitals in the nation’s capital. There, she said, doctors also consider the woman’s age and the ages of her children before agreeing to tubal ligations. She said for temporary contraceptive methods, it suffices if the woman says she spoke to her husband. Getting consent for tubal ligation, she added, is based in cultural and social norms that put decisions in the hands of husbands as their families.

Across Pakistan, however, men are free to have vasectomies without their wives’ consent. Physicians said men were not bound by that rule, though they acknowledged that vasectomies are not commonly sought by Pakistani men.

Dr Zaib Dahar strongly disagreed with the doctors who say these days it is not a problem and that science has improved now “although science is the same, the body is the same, the uterus is the same, there is no science in it.”

Rubina lives in a rural Sindh village with her three kids and her husband. She helps in the field and runs the house. She failed to carry three babies to term, which she attributes to l lifting too much weight during those pregnancies. All three ended with a lot of blood loss, making Rubina afraid to risk another.

In December, she said she had decided to have her tubes tied.

The Sindh Reproductive Act passed in 2019, stating that women have a right to make independent decisions about their reproductive rights and to have those decisions respected by others. It also calls for  integrated services, including family planning, to address maternal mortality and morbidity and strengthen the reproductive health care system. It encourages healthcare providers to help patients make informed choices in an environment of dignity and confidentiality.

Tunio said she was not aware of the Reproductive Act. Several doctors from other parts of Pakistan who were interviewed for this story didn’t know that there was no federal law requiring a husband’s consent for tubal ligations. That restriction is only codified in Punjab Province.

“This whole approach that providers take towards family planning is so judgemental,” said Sathar from the Population Council.

Latif, the reproductive rights advocate, said even she couldn’t get a tubal ligation without her husband’s consent. She knew she wanted her tubes tied after she gave birth to her second child, something she had informed her doctor about beforehand.  The doctor suggested she speak to the husband and confirmed that she had done so at a subsequent appointment.Twelve hours later, she went into labor and upon delivery, was asked one more time if she was sure and had informed her husband.  Despite Latif’s assurances, the doctorsent the anaesthesiologist to ask the husband if he was aware that his wife would be getting a tubal ligation.

Latif later asked the doctor “You know you don't need his consent right?” And the doctor said she understood that, but she wasn’t willing to put anybody in her staff at risk of being  harmed by an irate husband.

“I want you to really take that in,” Latif stressed, “because even at the most elite, even at the most progressive levels, when it comes to family planning, there is hesitation. And the reason for that is that our health care providers do not have the power or agency to be protected by law or culture.”

Dr Gul pointed out that in smaller cities like Larkana,it is likely that people know the doctor because she is from within the community. Those doctors are expected to follow the norms of the patriarchal society, one of which is that a man’s masculinity and virilityis directly related to the number of children he can produce.

Many men would feel threatened by a doctor who took that decision from them, she added. “You don't think there would be a backlash or reaction?,” she asked.

Dr Shershah Syed said he usually gets only the woman to sign a consent form. “She will decide if she wants a tubal ligation,” he said. But he conceded that if he suspects that the woman might get in trouble later, he tells her to ask her husband.

He said he has done up to 13 c-sections on one patient, and in such situations, he has done a tubal ligationbecause he knew the patient would die if they had another.

Family planning needs to be understood as a behavioural practice as opposed to a health care decision, Xaher said.

“It needs to be a part of visible practice that others do, and it needs to be linked to rewards and incentives for those who do it well.”

Prohibition of permanent method in religion

Salma and Hussain Baksh have seven children. The first time Salma gave birth, she needed two bottles of blood. The same thing happened the second time she gave birth. She suffers from hepatitis C, so her husband asked a scholar about spacing out births because of blood loss.

“So I had her take a break,” he said.

Hussain Baksh earns around 700 rupees (about $2.50) each day and some days he doesn't even earn that, making it difficult to provide for children.  Still, he sees children as “Allah’s gift.”

There is a common belief that Islam, which is the prevalent religion in the country, does not allow for permanent contraception. But not all religious scholars hold to that. Though Pakistani law is rooted in Islam, it does not prohibit permanent contraception for women or men.

Allama Muhammad Raghib Hussain Naeemi, the chairman of the Council of Islamic Ideology, said it is only permissible if a pregnancy is life threatening.The council is a constitutional body that advises the Parliament, the president, and provincial governments to ensure laws conform with the tenets of Islam. He said using temporary methods for birth spacing is allowed because it does not change the body's natural design.

“We are depriving any man or woman of this attribute that allows for them in the future in the birth of children,” he said, talking about using permanent contraception.

He added that in case a woman needs to make this decision for herself without letting the husband know, it is not allowed because it will harm the couples’ relationship and could lead to a divorce if he finds out.

“So why would she cause a breakup to save her life? She should break up with her husband earlier to save her life instead of cutting her tubes and then also causing the breakup.”

He said a woman cannot decide whether or not she needs the surgery. Only a doctor can determine that, based on whether it’s needed to save her life.

If there isn't a threat to her life, then one can do whatever they want with their body,” he said. “That’s not allowed.”

Javed Ghamidi, one of the very popular scholars in Pakistan agrees with the principle but acknowledges the caveats. “An individual cannot harm their body as in they can’t cut their arm or gouge their eyes out but if it is done with a reason behind it, it becomes legitimate,” he said.

A woman, then, can decide if she deems a tubal ligation necessary, he said. But he added that couples should make such decisions together because they shouldn’t hide things from each other.

Zahida, her husband Wazir and their eight children all live in one room. She does labour work and housework while taking care of her children. She wanted to have a tubal ligation after six children because she felt really weak, especially after the last birth. But her husband believed it would be a sin.

“Allah will give whatever is written for us,” he said.

She feared she would not survive another pregnancy and eventually was able to convince her husband, who relented and gave his consent.
